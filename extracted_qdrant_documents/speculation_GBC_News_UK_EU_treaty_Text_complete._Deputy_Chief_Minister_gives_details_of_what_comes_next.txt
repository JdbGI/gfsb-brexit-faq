Interview with the Deputy Chief Minister of Gibraltar, Dr Joseph Garcia

Dr Garcia:
First of all, as you know, this has been a long, complex and sometimes difficult negotiation. It has been very absorbing for those involved — primarily, from Gibraltar’s point of view, the Chief Minister, the Attorney General, myself, Daniel in Brussels, and other officials who have participated at different stages.

This last phase, since the political agreement was announced on 11 June, has involved a lot of remote work. But even recently, over the last few days and weeks, we have had situations where I was in my office, the Attorney General was in his, and the Chief Minister — unable to move — was lying on his left side on his sofa, trying to read text with one eye because of his medical condition. So it has been a difficult process in itself, sometimes complicated by other circumstances.

Q: Before we go on to what comes next, why have we heard first from the European Commission and the media — in this case the Gibraltar Chronicle — and not from the Gibraltar Government?

Dr Garcia:
This is an international treaty, and with an international treaty you have to be very careful about what you say and when you say it. There was an understanding that everyone would exercise restraint in making public statements.

Once the Commission makes a public statement — and they tell us this repeatedly — it becomes very difficult to contain information once it goes to the 27 member states. Twenty-six (excluding Spain, which is directly involved) suddenly receive the information, and it could end up anywhere. So at that point it is better simply to inform people that progress has been made.
Clearly, this is not the end of the process. It closes the chapter of negotiations, and now we move on to the next phase: the legal scrub of the treaty text and the technical review, ensuring consistency throughout. That can take several weeks and is only one phase among several still to come.

Q: What exactly is a legal scrub?

Dr Garcia:
A legal scrub involves lawyers from the two signatories — the United Kingdom and the European Union — with Gibraltar also involved, as throughout the process. They review the entire treaty text from beginning to end.
It includes technical matters such as numbering, headings, consistency between sections, and ensuring the treaty reads properly as a legal document. It can be quite a lengthy process.

Q: And then the treaty needs to be translated into all the different EU languages?

Dr Garcia:
Yes. Once the lawyers agree the final legal text — negotiated in English — it must be translated into the EU’s 24 official languages. Curiously, English is still one of those languages because of Ireland and Malta.
When translated, the German, French, Italian, or any other version has exactly the same legal weight as the negotiated English text. The EU has around 4,000 translators, about 2,000 of whom work for the Commission, and they will translate the treaty simultaneously into all languages. That also takes time.

Once that is complete, the treaty can be signed by the United Kingdom and the European Union. The signature confirms the text is correct.

Q: Will the signing be a quiet administrative affair or a public ceremony?

Dr Garcia:
We need to wait and see. It could be a routine signing, or it could be more ceremonial. We will see how that develops.

Q: Will the treaty text be published before ratification?

Dr Garcia:
Yes. Once there is a consolidated, agreed treaty text, it will be published. For example, when it goes before the Gibraltar Parliament for debate, the text will be public. That is standard practice for international treaties.

Q: What is the ratification process from there?

Dr Garcia:
There are two separate processes: one for the European Union and one for the United Kingdom.

For the UK side, the process begins in the Gibraltar Parliament. There will be a debate and discussion, and Parliament will signal to the UK whether Gibraltar is content with the treaty and urges the UK to ratify it.
Then the UK begins its own ratification process, which we are told can take up to 21 days, including discussions and questions in Westminster.

The EU process is separate. It first goes to the member states meeting in the European Council. Any member state could theoretically raise questions, although bear in mind the Commission — negotiating on behalf of the Council — has been briefing them throughout.
After the Council, it goes to the European Parliament, which must give its consent.

There are 720 MEPs. The largest group is the European People’s Party (Spain’s PP sits in that group) with 188 members. Next are the Socialists with about 136, and then the group called Patriots for Europe, with 84 MEPs, which includes Vox. So yes, there is arithmetic involved.

A treaty carrying the approval of the Commission and all member state governments would then need Parliament’s consent. We are not taking anything for granted and will ensure that our views are put to MEPs when the time comes.

Q: Could the European Parliament be a stumbling block?

Dr Garcia:
I wouldn’t describe it as a stumbling block — it is simply part of the process for any EU treaty. Theoretically, of course, anything could happen. But it is highly unlikely in circumstances where the treaty has been negotiated by the Commission, backed by all member states — including Spain, historically the main point of contention — and where MEPs will be voting on a text endorsed by their own governments.

Q: How does the European Parliament vote? Majority? Unanimity?

Dr Garcia:
The treaty requires a simple majority for consent.
MEPs sit not by nationality but by political group — there are eight groups across the ideological spectrum. We, and no doubt the EU side as well, will make representations to explain why we believe the treaty should be approved: because it is in the interests of the EU as a whole, of the wider UK–EU relationship, and of the people living in the region.

Q: Just to be clear: we will be able to see the treaty text after signature and before ratification?

Dr Garcia:
Yes. It will be published when it goes to Parliament, and that is part of the international treaty process.

Q: What sort of timeframe are we looking at? Bearing in mind Christmas, translation, signature…

Dr Garcia:
I’ve been in this business too long to risk giving a specific timeframe. I have outlined the whole process:

Gibraltar Parliament motion

UK parliamentary ratification (up to 21 days)

European Council approval

European Parliament consent
This treaty does not need to go before all national parliaments of the member states, which would have been a completely different situation.

Even after ratification, the treaty would not take effect immediately — there would be an implementation period.

Q: I still have to push you: ballpark, when might it reach the European Parliament?

Dr Garcia:
It is very difficult to say. All sides — the UK, Gibraltar, Spain, and the EU — want this to happen as quickly as possible. That is clear.
What I can say is that it will be next year, obviously, but I cannot say exactly when next year.
